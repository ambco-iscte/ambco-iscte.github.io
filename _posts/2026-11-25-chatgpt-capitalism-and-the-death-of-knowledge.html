No One Understands Anything

In recent years, Generative Artificial Intelligence has become nearly ubiquitous and the field of Education is still reeling from its impact (and will likely be doing so for a while longer). Educators everywhere are struggling with curricula that now feels outdated, assessment methods which offer little or no evidence of learning in the face of widespread Generative AI usage, and concerns of imminent, forced obsolescence of their field.

The responses to this could not be more varied. On one end, some educators have gone full analogue, returning to proctored pen-and-paper exams and enforcing stricter oral evaluations and vivas. On the other hand, some educators have welcomed AI with open arms, integrating it into as much of their teaching methodology as possible.

Regardless of personal stances on AI adoption or restriction, the overarching consensus seems to be that teaching needs to move away from detailed technical knowledge (which Generative AI can almost certainly perform in many fields) in favour of higher-level cognitive skills such as creativity, problem-solving, and critical thinking. However, this raises a concerning question I believe warrants discussion: how can we teach students to think critically if we don’t teach them the technical knowledge they need to be familiar enough with what we want them to think critically about? I specialise in programming education, and I struggle to understand how I could ever teach students to rationally and critically analyse program code if I don’t teach them how code works beforehand. Is critical thinking separable from, or does it contain, the acquisition of fundamental technical knowledge?

You may argue that this change is comparable to the change from machine language to higher-level programming languages. For instance, we can write good C code without fully understanding how it compiles to machine language or how each instruction is executed at the physical processor level. Likewise, I can write a readable, coherent essay without understanding the mechanisms of language development or how each of my words evolved from their Proto-Indo-European ancestors. However, I also do not have to deeply analyse or think very critically about a processor’s individual transistors or long-gone ancestral languages. When programming, the code I write directly maps to my intended final product (an executable program), and it’s possible to understand and reason about how that programs works by analysing the code (of course, barring very specific, high-performance computing scenarios where you may need to delve deeper into compiled machine language and processor architecture).

With AI, even this is abstracted. You can write a natural language prompt and get back a fully functioning program or professional-sounding essay, but that interaction in itself doesn’t tell you anything about how that program works or whether your essay correctly covers the point you’re trying to make. The generation of a valid output has been nearly completely separated from understanding what you’re generating.

Now, my point is as follows. Let’s say you have just enough domain knowledge to write an understandable prompt (Which is a closely related issue: can you write a good prompt if you don’t know enough about what you’re writing?). You do so, and get back good-looking and professional-sounding output. How can you check whether that output is actually acceptable if you don’t have the knowledge of how it works? Can you analyse and reason about a computer program if you can’t understand how the code works? Can you think critically about an essay written in a language which you can’t read or write?

Some of the more fervent AI adopters will mention how AI displays incredibly good performance and is therefore reliable enough that we can trust its outputs. Of course, it’s true that there are a great many tasks that Generative AI systems can already perform quite well. In the end, – and ethical concerns aside – we must ask ourselves if we are comfortable employing a machine we can’t understand to generate products and results we don’t care to understand. The “human in the loop” becomes a thing of the past? Alternatively, I’ve also been told that you could “validate” an AI output by asking another AI system about it. This is circular reasoning. You’d have to validate that AI’s explanation just the same, so you’d need to ask a third AI for a meta-explanation, and validate that by asking a fourth AI for a meta-meta-explanation, ad infinitum until you run out of turtles.

No One Learns Anything, Either

As a Ph.D. candidate researching programming education and an aspiring professor of Computer Science, the educational and associated long-term societal impacts of Generative AI on our students concern me immensely. Put simply, I believe this is the apparent problem:

  1. Generative AI can abstract technical knowledge and separate it from product creation.
  2. Educators move away from technical tasks in favour of critical analysis so that students may learn to, at least, think critically about AI-generated products.
  3. But, if students don’t have enough technical knowledge to understand those outputs, how are they supposed to reason critically about them?

Pedagogically, this is a disaster. At this point, would we even be teaching anything? Just let students input all your material into a Large Language Model (LLM) and have them memorise just enough to achieve enough marks in the course.

Unfortunately, quite commonly this is exactly what happens: students will be nearly entirely dependent on LLMs to study, and end up just memorising AI-generated explanations so that they can regurgitate just enough bullshit on their exams to pass their courses. It’s also not uncommon for educators to employ Generative AI to generate course materials and grade assignments and exams. In the end, no one really does anything nor does anyone really learn anything.

And Everyone Is Conditioned Not to Care

Many educators will attribute this to personal moral failures or character flaws in students. “Bah, they should learn some responsibility and just put in the work!” I don’t think this is fair. It is very difficult to instill in people the intrinsic motivation required to put in the work just for the sake of learning. The “reward” (learning, knowledge acquisition, personal growth) is too intangible to serve as adequate motivation for most people. Like the term suggests, you can’t force intrinsic motivation to be there when it just isn’t. As a professor of mine once said, “you can’t expect to convert the unconvertible.”

It’s not surprising that students are tempted to use Generative AI to pass their courses without really learning the material: after all, the outcome is the same (passing courses, getting a degree) with much less effort. This is where I think a critical observation has to be made. Sure, it’s easy to dismiss the problem as “students not wanting to put in the effort”. That is what it is, but we should think about why this is the case. Are we to believe everyone is just inherently lazy for the sake of being lazy? I would prefer not to.

I believe that, ultimately, this is a manifestation of the current state of our society. Capitalism and neoliberalism run rampant worldwide, and everyone seems to have a strange fetish for optimal productivity and profit maximisation. It’s no surprise that the ultimate goal is to produce a working end product as fast as possible and with as little (economic) effort as possible. Obviously, this is not a recent paradigm. In 1903, Frederick Taylor wrote that “The art of management has been defined, "as knowing exactly what you want men to do, and then seeing that they do it in the best and cheapest way".”

Regardless, it’s no wonder that this motivates students to do what they do. We have all been brought up in a society which seems to value results and profit above all else. Moreover, students are aware that this is likely the case in the workforce that they’re aiming to become a part of. The application of “I only care about the end result” to education becomes completely obvious.

Academia aside, the delegation of learning tasks to generative AI models stunts cognitive development and puts literacy, critical thinking, and creativity at risk. In a society plagued by misinformation, do we think it's wise to raise a generation of workers and members of society who can't reason critically about the news or the things they read online?

I’ll finish with a markedly unpopular opinion (or maybe my peers are out there, silent) within the education community: trying to teach “responsible AI usage” is naïve and ultimately a losing battle. Education is not an isolated system with self-contained problems which can be solved purely through the motivated, good-intentioned effort of educators. We can hold seminars on ethical AI and the importance of learning, teach courses on ethical AI usage, adopt new pedagogical tools and methods, or enforce AI regulation policies. As long as the rot of capitalism continues seeping into the field, it will remain very difficult to convince students to behave in a way that, out there “in the real world”, doesn’t seem to matter too much.

And this sucks, and I don’t think anyone knows what to do about it, and it frustrates me so I wrote a long stream-of-consciousness blog post about it. Goodbye.
