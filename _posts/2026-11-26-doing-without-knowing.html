Understanding Your Own Work (Optional)

In recent years, Generative Artificial Intelligence has become nearly ubiquitous and the field of Education is still reeling from its impact (and will likely be doing so for a while longer). Educators everywhere are struggling with curricula that now feels outdated, assessment methods which offer little or no evidence of learning in the face of widespread Generative AI usage, and concerns of imminent, forced obsolescence of their field.

The responses to this could not be more varied. On one end, some educators have gone full analogue, returning to proctored pen-and-paper exams and enforcing stricter oral evaluations and vivas. On the other hand, some educators have welcomed AI with open arms, integrating it into as much of their teaching methodology as possible.

Regardless of personal stances on AI adoption or restriction, the overarching consensus seems to be that teaching needs to move away from detailed technical knowledge (which Generative AI can almost certainly perform in many fields) in favour of higher-level cognitive skills such as creativity, problem-solving, and critical thinking. However, this raises a concerning question I believe warrants discussion: how can we teach students to think critically if we don’t teach them the technical knowledge they need to be familiar enough with what we want them to think critically about? I specialise in programming education, and I struggle to understand how I could ever teach students to rationally and critically analyse program code if I don’t teach them how code works beforehand. Is critical thinking separable from, or does it contain, the acquisition of fundamental technical knowledge?

You may argue that this change is comparable to the change from machine language to higher-level programming languages. For instance, we can write good C code without fully understanding how it compiles to machine language or how each instruction is executed at the physical processor level. Likewise, I can write a readable, coherent essay without understanding the mechanisms of language development or how each of my words evolved from their Proto-Indo-European ancestors. However, I also do not have to deeply analyse or think very critically about a processor’s individual transistors or long-gone ancestral languages. When programming, the code I write directly maps to my intended final product (an executable program), and it’s possible to understand and reason about how that programs works by analysing the code.{% footnote %} Barring very specific, high-performance computing scenarios where you may need to delve deeper into compiled machine language and processor architecture. {% endfootnote %}

Aha! But if you don't fully understand the compiler it's built on, then you can't claim you truly understand your own program either!

Well, yes, you won't have the complete pool of knowledge that has led up to your program, but you can at least understand the code layer. With AI, even this is abstracted. You can write a natural language prompt and get back a fully functioning program or professional-sounding essay, but that interaction in itself doesn’t tell you anything about how that program works or whether your essay correctly covers the point you’re trying to make. The generation of a valid output has been nearly completely separated from understanding what you’re generating.

Let’s say you have just enough domain knowledge to write an understandable prompt.{% footnote %} Which is an issue in and of itself: can you write a good prompt if you don’t know enough about what you’re writing? {% endfootnote %} You do so, and get back good-looking and professional-sounding output. How can you check whether that output is actually acceptable if you don’t have the knowledge of how it works? Can you analyse and reason about a computer program if you can’t understand how the code works? Can you think critically about an essay written in a language you can’t read or write?

Some of the more fervent AI adopters will mention how AI displays increasingly good performance and is therefore reliable enough that we can trust its outputs. Of course, it’s true that there are a great many tasks which Generative AI systems can already perform quite well. In the end, – and ethical concerns aside – we must ask ourselves if we are comfortable employing a machine we can’t understand to generate products and results we don’t care to understand. Does the “human in the loop” become a thing of the past? I’ve also been told that you could “validate” an AI output by asking another AI system about it, but this is circular reasoning. You’d have to validate that AI’s explanation just the same, so you’d need to ask a third AI for a meta-explanation, and validate that by asking a fourth AI for a meta-meta-explanation, ad infinitum until you run out of turtles.

Our Fight as Educators

As a Ph.D. candidate researching programming education and an aspiring professor of Computer Science, the educational and associated long-term societal impacts of Generative AI on our students concern me immensely. Put simply, I believe this is the apparent problem:

  1. Generative AI can abstract technical knowledge and separate it from product creation.
  2. Educators move away from technical tasks in favour of critical analysis so that students may learn to, at least, think critically about AI-generated products.
  3. But, if students don’t have enough technical knowledge to understand those outputs, how are they supposed to reason critically about them?

Pedagogically, this is a disaster. At this point, would we even be teaching anything? Just let students input all your material into a Large Language Model (LLM) and have them memorise just enough to achieve enough marks in the course.

Unfortunately, quite commonly this is exactly what happens: students will be nearly entirely dependent on LLMs to study, and end up just memorising AI-generated explanations so that they can regurgitate just enough bullshit on their exams to pass their courses. It’s also not uncommon for educators to employ Generative AI to generate course materials and grade assignments and exams. In the end, no one really does anything nor does anyone really learn much of anything.

As educators, we don't yet have a definitive answer to this problem, and I don't believe anyone else does, either. A definitive answer may be impossible to achieve. I don't think naïvely trying to step over fundamental, technical tasks to skip straight to critical thinking is a good way to approach this.{% footnote %} Even teaching "ethical AI usage" is questionable, for reasons which I may come to discuss but which are well outside the scope of this article. {% endfootnote %} This article has been my argument for this.

There truly is a lot of reflection to be done. (And please, let's not try to get there by asking ChatGPT.)
